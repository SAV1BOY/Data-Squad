# Strategy for Data Conference Speaking

## Purpose

Conference speaking is one of the highest-leverage activities for building credibility, recruiting talent, and expanding your professional network in the data field. This guide covers how to choose conferences, craft proposals, prepare talks, and maximize the return on your speaking investment.

## Conference Selection

### Tier 1: Must-Apply Conferences
Large conferences with broad data/analytics audiences. High visibility, competitive selection.

**Examples:**
- Measure Camp (unconference format, highly interactive)
- dbt Coalesce (analytics engineering focused)
- Supermetrics Summit / Marketing Analytics Summit
- LookML Developer Conference / Looker Join
- Tableau Conference
- Data Council
- Strata Data & AI Conference

**Apply if:** You have a polished, original talk with clear takeaways and a compelling narrative.

### Tier 2: Regional and Specialized Conferences
Smaller conferences with focused audiences. Easier to get accepted, deeper engagement.

**Examples:**
- Local data meetup groups (Data Science [City], Analytics [City])
- Industry-specific analytics events (SaaStr for SaaS, eTail for e-commerce)
- Tool-specific user conferences (Amplitude Amplify, Mixpanel Signal)
- Academic-industry crossover events (KDD applied track, CIKM industry day)

**Apply if:** You have a solid talk and want to practice before larger stages, or your content is niche enough to benefit from a specialized audience.

### Tier 3: Internal and Community Events
Private or semi-private events with high-trust audiences.

**Examples:**
- Company all-hands or tech talks
- Customer advisory board presentations
- Slack community AMAs (Locally Optimistic, dbt Community)
- Podcast guest appearances
- Webinar series

**Apply if:** You are building your speaking skills and want a lower-stakes environment to practice.

## Crafting a Talk Proposal

### The Proposal Formula

A winning proposal answers four questions:

1. **What will the audience learn?** (The takeaway)
2. **Why should they care?** (The stakes)
3. **Why are you credible on this topic?** (The authority)
4. **What makes this talk different?** (The angle)

### Proposal Template

**Title:** [Specific, benefit-oriented, slightly provocative]
- Good: "Why We Stopped Tracking 80% of Our Metrics (And Made Better Decisions)"
- Bad: "Our Journey to Better Analytics"

**Abstract (100-200 words):**
- Paragraph 1: The problem or question this talk addresses. Make it relatable.
- Paragraph 2: What the audience will learn -- 3 specific takeaways.
- Paragraph 3: Why you are uniquely qualified to give this talk (brief).

**Outline (bullet points):**
- 4-6 sections with time estimates
- Show the narrative arc: setup -> tension -> insight -> resolution -> takeaway

**Speaker bio (50-100 words):**
- Focus on relevant experience, not job titles
- Include one surprising or memorable detail
- Mention previous speaking experience if applicable

### Topics That Get Accepted

| Topic Type | Why It Works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| War story with lessons | Authentic, relatable, actionable | "How a timezone bug cost us $800K" |
| Methodology deep-dive | Fills knowledge gaps | "Implementing CUPED for faster experiments" |
| Contrarian position with evidence | Creates engagement and debate | "Why NPS is the wrong metric for SaaS" |
| Tool/process transformation | Practical and immediately applicable | "Moving from dashboard chaos to decision support" |
| Framework introduction | Organizes complex thinking | "The 4-level experimentation maturity model" |

### Topics That Get Rejected

- Thinly veiled product pitches
- Abstract theory without practical application
- "We built a thing" without lessons learned
- Topics that are too broad ("Analytics Best Practices")
- Talks that require the audience to use a specific tool

## Preparing the Talk

### Talk Structure (30-minute format)

| Section | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 2 min | Capture attention with a surprising fact, question, or story |
| Context | 3 min | Set up the problem and why the audience should care |
| Core content (3 sections) | 18 min | The substance -- insights, methods, results |
| Key takeaways | 3 min | Summarize what the audience should remember and do |
| Q&A | 4 min | Engage with the audience directly |

### Slide Design Principles

- One idea per slide. If you have two ideas, use two slides.
- Minimize text. If you are reading your slides, you have too much text.
- Use large, annotated charts. The audience cannot read a 12-column table from row 20.
- Include a "you should try this" slide with concrete next steps.
- Number your slides (e.g., "12/30") so the audience knows where you are in the talk.

### Rehearsal Process

1. **Outline rehearsal**: Talk through the structure without slides. Does the story flow?
2. **Full rehearsal (alone)**: Present with slides, time yourself. Cut ruthlessly if over time.
3. **Practice audience rehearsal**: Present to 2-3 colleagues. Ask: What was unclear? What was the key takeaway? What would you cut?
4. **Final rehearsal**: Incorporate feedback. Time yourself again. You should be 2-3 minutes under your time limit to allow for Q&A and natural pacing.

## Maximizing Return on Speaking

### Before the Talk
- Announce the talk on LinkedIn and Twitter 2 weeks before
- Prepare a one-page handout or resource list to share with attendees
- Research the audience: What is their expertise level? What are they struggling with?

### During the Talk
- Start with a question or poll to engage the audience immediately
- Share your contact information and resource links early (not just at the end)
- Speak to individuals, not "the audience" -- make eye contact, respond to reactions

### After the Talk
- Post slides and key takeaways on LinkedIn within 24 hours
- Write a blog post expanding on the talk (this doubles your content output)
- Connect with people who asked questions or approached you afterward
- Send a follow-up message to the conference organizers thanking them

### Building a Speaking Pipeline

- Apply to 3-5 conferences per quarter
- Accept that rejection is common -- even experienced speakers are rejected from most conferences
- Track your applications, acceptances, and rejections to identify which topics and conferences are the best fit
- Build relationships with conference organizers. Attend conferences as a participant before applying as a speaker.
- Ask for feedback on rejected proposals and improve them for the next submission

## Measuring Speaking Impact

| Metric | How to Measure | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Audience engagement | Q&A quality, post-talk conversations | 3+ substantive questions |
| Content amplification | Social shares of slides/post | 50+ shares |
| Network growth | New connections within 1 week | 10+ quality connections |
| Inbound opportunities | Speaking invitations, collaboration requests | 1+ per talk |
| Talent pipeline | Candidates who mention your talks | Track in recruiting |

## Key Principles

1. **Teach, do not sell.** The moment your talk feels like a pitch, you lose the audience.
2. **Share real numbers.** Specific results ($X saved, Y% improvement) are 10x more compelling than vague claims.
3. **Be honest about failures.** Audiences trust speakers who share what went wrong, not just what went right.
4. **Respect the audience's time.** End on time or early. Never run over.
5. **Start small and build.** Your first talk should be at a meetup, not a keynote stage. Build skills incrementally.
6. **Repurpose relentlessly.** One conference talk can become a blog post, a LinkedIn series, a newsletter issue, and a team training session.
