# Customer Centricity Score

## Purpose

Assess how genuinely customer-centric the organization is, using Fader's definition: recognizing and acting on the fact that not all customers are equal, and aligning strategy, structure, and metrics around differential customer value. This is not about being "nice to customers" -- it is about organizing around customer heterogeneity.

---

## Checklist Items

### 1. The Organization Can Rank Customers by Value
- **Pass:** There is a live, queryable ranking of customers by current and predicted value, accessible to teams that make customer-facing decisions.
- **Fail:** No customer value ranking exists, or it is a one-time project sitting in an archived spreadsheet.

### 2. Resource Allocation Differs by Customer Value
- **Pass:** High-value customers receive more attention (dedicated CSMs, faster support, personalized offers). Low-value customers are served through scalable, lower-cost channels.
- **Fail:** All customers receive identical treatment regardless of value, or resource allocation is based on whoever asks loudest.

### 3. Product Development Considers Customer Value Input
- **Pass:** Feature requests and product priorities are weighted by the value of the customers requesting them, not just by request volume.
- **Fail:** Product roadmap is driven by feature request count or by the loudest individual customer, regardless of value.

### 4. Financial Reporting Includes Customer-Level P&L
- **Pass:** The organization can produce a customer-level (or segment-level) profit and loss statement, accounting for revenue, cost to serve, and cost to acquire.
- **Fail:** Financial reporting is product-centric or channel-centric only, with no customer-level view.

### 5. Organizational Structure Has Customer Dimension
- **Pass:** At least part of the organization is structured around customer segments (e.g., enterprise vs. SMB teams) rather than purely around products or functions.
- **Fail:** The entire organization is structured by product line or function with no customer-segment orientation.

### 6. Acquisition Strategy Is Informed by Best-Customer Profiles
- **Pass:** Marketing uses profiles of the highest-value existing customers to target acquisition, seeking "more of the best" rather than "more of anyone."
- **Fail:** Acquisition targets the broadest possible audience with no value-based targeting.

### 7. Churn Analysis Distinguishes Value Lost
- **Pass:** Churn reports show not just the number of customers lost but the value lost, distinguishing between losing a high-value customer and a low-value one.
- **Fail:** Churn is reported as a single rate with no value weighting.

### 8. Incentive Structures Reward Value, Not Just Volume
- **Pass:** Sales and CS compensation includes components tied to customer quality (CLV, retention of high-value accounts) not just new logos or total revenue.
- **Fail:** Sales is compensated purely on new revenue with no regard for customer quality or long-term value.

### 9. Customer Data Is Unified Across Touchpoints
- **Pass:** There is a single customer record (or reliably joined view) spanning sales, support, product usage, and marketing interactions, enabling a complete value picture.
- **Fail:** Customer data is siloed by function, making it impossible to compute holistic customer value.

### 10. Leadership Speaks in Customer-Value Language
- **Pass:** Board decks and executive reviews include customer-level metrics (CLV, segment migration, value concentration). Leaders reference customer value in strategic decisions.
- **Fail:** Leadership discussions are entirely about product revenue, channel performance, or aggregate growth with no customer-level lens.
