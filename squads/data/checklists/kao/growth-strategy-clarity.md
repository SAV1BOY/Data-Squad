# Growth Strategy Clarity

## Purpose

Audit whether the growth strategy answers the most important question: "Where should we hit first?" Following Emily Kao's emphasis on strategic clarity, this checklist ensures the team has identified the single biggest lever and is not diffusing effort across too many fronts simultaneously.

---

## Checklist Items

### 1. The Biggest Growth Lever Is Explicitly Named
- **Pass:** The team can state in one sentence which lever (acquisition channel, activation step, retention driver, monetization model) they believe will create the most impact in the next quarter.
- **Fail:** The team has a list of "priorities" but cannot rank them. Everything is equally important, which means nothing is.

### 2. The Choice Is Backed by Evidence
- **Pass:** The lever selection is supported by quantitative analysis (funnel data, cohort analysis, market sizing) or validated qualitative evidence (customer interviews, competitive analysis).
- **Fail:** The choice is based on intuition, executive directive, or copying a competitor without understanding if the same dynamics apply.

### 3. Alternative Levers Were Evaluated and Deprioritized
- **Pass:** The team considered at least 3 alternative strategies, documented why each was deprioritized, and can explain the trade-offs.
- **Fail:** The current strategy is the only one considered, or alternatives were discussed informally but never documented.

### 4. The Strategy Has a Time Horizon and Success Criteria
- **Pass:** The strategy has a defined time window (e.g., "next 90 days") and specific success criteria (e.g., "activation rate from 25% to 35%"). There is a clear answer to "how will we know this worked?"
- **Fail:** The strategy is open-ended with no timeline or measurable success criteria.

### 5. Resource Allocation Matches the Strategy
- **Pass:** At least 60% of the growth team's capacity is allocated to the identified biggest lever. Headcount, engineering time, and budget reflect the stated priority.
- **Fail:** The strategy says "focus on activation" but 80% of engineering time goes to new features unrelated to activation.

### 6. The Strategy Is Communicated Simply
- **Pass:** The growth strategy can be explained in under 2 minutes to a new team member or a stakeholder. Complexity has been distilled, not hidden.
- **Fail:** The strategy requires a 40-slide deck to explain, indicating it is either too complex or not yet clear in the team's own mind.

### 7. Dependencies Are Identified
- **Pass:** External dependencies (platform changes, partner agreements, data infrastructure) that could block the strategy are identified with mitigation plans.
- **Fail:** The strategy assumes everything will go smoothly with no consideration of blockers.

### 8. The Strategy Accounts for the Current Stage
- **Pass:** The strategy is appropriate for the company's current stage (pre-PMF, post-PMF scaling, mature optimization). Pre-PMF companies focus on finding fit; post-PMF companies optimize levers.
- **Fail:** A pre-PMF company is scaling paid acquisition, or a mature company is chasing product pivots instead of optimizing its core.

### 9. Leading Indicators Are Identified for Early Signal
- **Pass:** The team has identified leading indicators (measurable within 2-4 weeks) that will provide early signal on whether the strategy is working before the lagging success metric moves.
- **Fail:** The team will not know if the strategy is working until the quarter is over.

### 10. There Is a Plan B
- **Pass:** If the primary strategy does not show progress within a defined window, there is a documented pivot plan identifying the next lever to attack.
- **Fail:** The team will figure out what to do next if this does not work, but has not thought about it in advance.
