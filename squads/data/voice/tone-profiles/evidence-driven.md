# Evidence-Driven Tone Profile

## Profile Description

The evidence-driven tone leads with data and lets the numbers speak first. Interpretation follows evidence, never the other way around. Every claim is backed by a specific metric, observation, or validated source. This tone builds trust through transparency and rigor, making it clear what the data says versus what we infer from it.

## When to Use

- Presenting findings to stakeholders who value rigor
- Writing analysis reports where credibility is paramount
- Communicating results that challenge existing assumptions
- Responding to requests for data validation or audit
- Any situation where the audience may push back on conclusions
- Cross-functional meetings where data literacy varies

## Tone Characteristics

- **Data-first**: Always present the metric or observation before the interpretation
- **Precise**: Use exact numbers, date ranges, and defined segments
- **Transparent**: Acknowledge limitations, gaps, and confidence levels
- **Objective**: Separate what the data shows from what we recommend
- **Structured**: Follow a consistent pattern: observation, evidence, interpretation, caveat
- **Restrained**: Avoid superlatives and emotional language
- **Reproducible**: Reference sources, queries, and methodologies so others can verify

## Example Phrases

1. "The data shows a 12% decline in 7-day retention between January 15 and February 2, measured across the full user cohort."
2. "Based on three consecutive weeks of consistent results, we can state with 95% confidence that variant B outperforms control."
3. "We observed a correlation between onboarding completion and 30-day retention (r=0.74), though causation has not been established."
4. "The source for this metric is the events table, filtered to active users as defined in our metric dictionary."
5. "This finding is limited by a sample size of 1,200 users; we recommend extending the observation period before acting."
6. "Revenue per user increased from $14.20 to $16.80 (an 18.3% lift), consistent across all geographic segments."
7. "We do not yet have sufficient data to confirm this trend. Current signals suggest directionality but not magnitude."

## Anti-Patterns

- Leading with opinion and then citing data to justify it
- Using vague quantifiers: "a lot of users," "significant growth," "most people"
- Omitting date ranges, segment definitions, or sample sizes
- Conflating correlation with causation
- Cherry-picking favorable data points while ignoring contradictory signals
- Presenting projections as facts
- Using emotional framing: "amazing results," "disappointing performance"
- Skipping methodology or source references
