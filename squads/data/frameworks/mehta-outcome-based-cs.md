# Mehta Outcome-Based CS

## Overview

Outcome-Based Customer Success is the shift from measuring and managing CS team activities ("we did
X") to measuring and managing customer outcomes ("the customer achieved Y"). Traditional CS measures
inputs: meetings held, QBRs conducted, emails sent, playbooks executed. Outcome-based CS measures
outputs: did the customer achieve the business objectives they purchased the product to accomplish?
This reframing transforms CS from a cost center that tracks its own busyness into a strategic
function that demonstrates measurable customer impact.

## Origin

Advocated by **Nick Mehta** through Gainsight's evolution of the customer success discipline.
Mehta observed that the first generation of CS was relationship-driven (dedicated CSMs maintaining
relationships), the second was process-driven (playbooks, automation, health scores), and the third
must be outcome-driven (measuring and delivering on customer objectives). This evolution reflects
the maturation of CS from an intuitive practice to a data-driven discipline.

## Core Model

### The Activity-Outcome Spectrum

| CS Activity (Input) | Customer Outcome (Output) |
|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Conducted onboarding sessions | Customer achieved first value milestone |
| Held quarterly business reviews | Customer can articulate ROI of the product |
| Sent usage reports | Customer increased efficiency by measurable amount |
| Resolved support tickets | Customer can self-serve without support dependency |
| Delivered training sessions | Customer team has adopted key workflows |
| Executed expansion playbook | Customer expanded because they needed more value |

### The Outcome Framework

**Step 1: Define Customer Desired Outcomes (CDOs)**
Before you can measure outcomes, you must know what outcomes customers seek. CDOs are the business
objectives customers are trying to achieve with your product.

Sources for CDOs:
- Sales process documentation (why did the customer buy?)
- Onboarding discovery conversations
- QBR discussions about business objectives
- Industry knowledge about common challenges

CDO categories:
- **Efficiency outcomes:** "Reduce time spent on X by Y%"
- **Revenue outcomes:** "Increase pipeline by Y% through better targeting"
- **Risk outcomes:** "Reduce compliance violations to zero"
- **Experience outcomes:** "Improve employee satisfaction with internal tools"

**Step 2: Map Outcomes to Observable Indicators**
For each CDO, identify what you would observe in product usage data if the outcome were being
achieved. These are outcome indicators, not activity metrics.

Example CDO: "Reduce time from data request to delivered insight."
- Outcome indicators: Average time from data request to dashboard creation decreasing over time,
  number of self-serve reports increasing, data team support tickets decreasing.

**Step 3: Establish Baselines**
Measure outcome indicators at the start of the customer relationship (or at the current point, if
transitioning to outcome-based CS). Without baselines, you cannot demonstrate improvement.

**Step 4: Track Progress Against Outcomes**
Create customer-level outcome dashboards that show progress toward CDOs. Share these in QBRs
instead of (or in addition to) usage reports.

**Step 5: Connect Outcomes to Business Value**
Translate outcome achievement into business value: time saved x hourly cost = dollar impact.
Revenue influenced x win rate = pipeline impact. This enables the customer to articulate ROI to
their own stakeholders.

### The Outcome Maturity Model

**Level 1: Activity-Driven CS**
- CSMs track their own activities (calls, emails, meetings)
- Success is measured by activity completion
- Customer value is assumed, not measured
- Common metric: "100% of customers received a QBR this quarter"

**Level 2: Engagement-Driven CS**
- CS tracks customer engagement (logins, feature usage, adoption)
- Success is measured by engagement levels
- Engagement is used as a proxy for value delivery
- Common metric: "DAU/MAU ratio is 45%"

**Level 3: Outcome-Driven CS**
- CS defines and tracks customer desired outcomes
- Success is measured by outcome achievement
- CSMs are aligned to customer objectives, not internal processes
- Common metric: "78% of customers achieved their primary outcome this quarter"

**Level 4: Prescriptive Outcome CS**
- CS uses data to predict which outcomes are achievable and prescribe the path
- Success is measured by outcome achievement rate and efficiency
- CSMs are supported by data models that recommend interventions
- Common metric: "Customers following prescribed paths achieve outcomes 2.3x faster"

## Application Steps

### Step 1: Audit Current Measurement
Classify every metric your CS team currently tracks as either activity, engagement, or outcome.
Most teams discover that 80%+ of their metrics are activities or engagement, with few true outcome
metrics.

### Step 2: Interview Customers About Desired Outcomes
Do not assume you know what customers want to achieve. Ask them. In the next 20 customer
conversations, ask: "What business objective were you trying to achieve when you purchased our
product? How would you know you had achieved it?"

### Step 3: Create an Outcome Taxonomy
Group customer desired outcomes into categories. Most products have 3-7 common CDO categories that
cover the majority of customers. Create clear definitions for each.

### Step 4: Map Indicators for Each Outcome
For each CDO category, identify the product usage data, customer-reported data, and business data
that would indicate progress. Prioritize indicators that are automatically collectible over those
requiring manual input.

### Step 5: Build Outcome Dashboards
Create customer-facing dashboards that show progress toward their specific CDOs. These replace
generic usage reports with personalized outcome tracking.

### Step 6: Restructure QBRs Around Outcomes
Transform quarterly business reviews from "here is your usage data" to "here is your progress
toward the outcomes you care about, and here is what we recommend next."

### Step 7: Align CSM Incentives
Shift CSM compensation and evaluation from activity metrics (meetings held, playbooks completed)
to outcome metrics (customer outcomes achieved, NRR within portfolio).

### Step 8: Feed Outcomes Back to Product
Aggregate outcome data across customers to inform product development. If many customers are not
achieving a specific outcome, the product may need improvement, not just the CS process.

## Key Distinctions

- **Outcomes are customer-defined, not vendor-defined.** The vendor does not get to define what
  success means. The customer does. The vendor's job is to measure and deliver against the
  customer's definition.
- **Engagement is not an outcome.** A customer who logs in daily but does not achieve their
  business objective is an engaged failure. Engagement is a means, not an end.
- **Activity completion is not outcome delivery.** Conducting an onboarding is an activity.
  The customer being able to independently use the product is an outcome.
- **Outcomes require a baseline.** "Our customer's data team is more efficient" is meaningless
  without "compared to what." Baselines enable measurement.
- **Outcome measurement is harder than activity measurement.** Activities are easy to track (did
  the CSM hold the meeting?). Outcomes require connecting product data to business impact. This
  difficulty is why most organizations have not made the shift.

## Pitfalls

1. **Defining outcomes too broadly.** "Customer success" is not a measurable outcome. "Customer
   reduced time-to-insight from 5 days to 2 days" is.
2. **Assuming engagement equals outcomes.** High usage does not mean the customer achieved their
   objective. It may mean they are struggling to achieve it.
3. **Measuring outcomes the customer does not care about.** If you define outcomes internally
   without customer input, you may be measuring the wrong things.
4. **Insufficient data infrastructure.** Outcome measurement requires connecting product usage
   data to customer business data. Without integration, outcome tracking devolves into manual
   surveys and self-reporting.
5. **CSM resistance to outcome accountability.** CSMs who are accustomed to being evaluated on
   activities may resist outcome-based evaluation, especially when outcomes are influenced by
   factors outside their control.
6. **Neglecting leading indicators.** Outcome achievement often takes months. Without leading
   indicators (engagement patterns that predict outcomes), CSMs cannot intervene early enough.
7. **Ignoring the "so what" for the customer.** Outcome data must be translated into business
   impact that the customer's stakeholders care about. "Feature adoption increased 40%" matters
   less than "your team saves 200 hours per month."
