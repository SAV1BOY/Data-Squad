# Constraint-Led Prioritization

## Purpose

Audit whether prioritization is driven by real constraints rather than by wish lists. Kao argues that the best growth teams do not try to do everything -- they identify the binding constraint and focus there. Constraints force strong choices, and strong choices produce outsized results.

---

## Checklist Items

### 1. The Binding Constraint Is Identified
- **Pass:** The team can name the single biggest constraint limiting growth right now (e.g., "activation rate is 18% -- every acquisition dollar is 82% wasted" or "engineering capacity limits us to 2 experiments per sprint").
- **Fail:** The team lists 10 problems but cannot identify which one, if solved, would unlock the most progress.

### 2. The Constraint Is Validated, Not Assumed
- **Pass:** The constraint is backed by data or evidence (funnel analysis, capacity audit, market research), not just a feeling or an inherited assumption.
- **Fail:** The team "knows" the constraint from experience but has never validated it with current data.

### 3. Prioritization Flows from the Constraint
- **Pass:** The top 3 priorities on the team's roadmap directly address the binding constraint. Work that does not address the constraint is explicitly deprioritized.
- **Fail:** The roadmap includes work on the constraint alongside 10 other initiatives, diluting focus.

### 4. Saying "No" Is Documented
- **Pass:** There is a documented list of things the team has chosen NOT to do this quarter, with the reason (not aligned with constraint). Saying no is an active decision.
- **Fail:** The team never explicitly says no. Work accumulates by addition without subtraction.

### 5. Resource Constraints Are Honestly Assessed
- **Pass:** The team has a realistic model of its available capacity (engineering hours, design bandwidth, data support) and plans work within that capacity, not beyond it.
- **Fail:** The plan assumes infinite capacity, leading to chronic overcommitment and under-delivery.

### 6. Time-Based Constraints Are Identified
- **Pass:** If there are time-sensitive constraints (seasonal windows, platform policy changes, competitive moves), they are identified and the plan accounts for them.
- **Fail:** The plan is time-agnostic, missing windows of opportunity or being blindsided by deadlines.

### 7. Constraint Removal Has a Measurable Outcome
- **Pass:** The team has defined what success looks like if the constraint is relieved (e.g., "activation rate moves from 18% to 30%"). This target is specific and time-bound.
- **Fail:** The team works on the constraint without a clear definition of what "constraint removed" looks like.

### 8. The Constraint Is Re-Evaluated After Progress
- **Pass:** When the constraint is partially or fully relieved, the team re-evaluates to find the next binding constraint. The bottleneck shifts, and so does the focus.
- **Fail:** The team continues working on the old constraint out of inertia even after it is no longer the bottleneck.

### 9. Constraints Are Communicated to Stakeholders
- **Pass:** Leadership understands the binding constraint and why other requests are deprioritized. The constraint framing helps stakeholders accept trade-offs.
- **Fail:** Stakeholders do not know why their requests are not being addressed, leading to frustration and end-runs.

### 10. Trade-Off Decisions Are Recorded
- **Pass:** When the team makes a trade-off (doing A instead of B because of a constraint), the decision and its rationale are logged for future reference and learning.
- **Fail:** Trade-offs are made in meetings and forgotten, making it impossible to learn from past prioritization decisions.
