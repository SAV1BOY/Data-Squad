# How Brands Grow - Byron Sharp (2010)

## Book Info

- **Title:** How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know
- **Author:** Byron Sharp
- **Publisher:** Oxford University Press
- **Year:** 2010 (updated edition 2015)
- **ISBN:** 978-0195573565
- **Pages:** 246
- **Context:** Sharp is a professor of marketing science and director of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute at the University of South Australia, the world's largest center for research on marketing. The book synthesizes decades of empirical research (building on Andrew Ehrenberg's work) to challenge conventional marketing wisdom. It is deliberately provocative, arguing that most of what marketers believe about brand loyalty, segmentation, and differentiation is empirically wrong.

## Core Thesis

Brand growth comes primarily from increasing market penetration (the number of people who buy the brand), not from increasing loyalty among existing buyers. Most marketing "strategy" -- targeting loyal segments, building deep relationships, differentiating on emotional benefits -- is either ineffective or counterproductive. The empirical laws of marketing show that: all brands share the same customers to varying degrees, brand loyalty is largely a statistical artifact of market share, and growth requires making the brand easier to buy for more people through mental availability (being thought of in buying situations) and physical availability (being easy to find and purchase).

## Key Concepts

### 1. Double Jeopardy Law
Small brands suffer twice: they have fewer buyers AND those buyers are slightly less loyal (buy less frequently). This is not because small brands are inferior; it is a mathematical property of buyer behavior. The practical implication: you cannot grow by making existing buyers more loyal. Growth requires acquiring more buyers, which automatically increases measured "loyalty" as a side effect of market share gains.

### 2. Mental Availability
The probability that a brand comes to mind in a buying situation. Mental availability is built through: distinctive brand assets (logo, color, jingle, tagline), broad reach (reaching all category buyers, not just "your segment"), and consistent presence over time. Mental availability is not the same as awareness (which is binary); it is about being recalled in the right context at the right moment.

### 3. Physical Availability
The ease with which a brand can be found and purchased. Distribution breadth, shelf placement, online discoverability, and purchase friction all determine physical availability. Many brands invest heavily in advertising (mental availability) while neglecting distribution (physical availability), which is often the bigger constraint on growth.

### 4. The Duplication of Purchase Law
Brands within a category share customers in proportion to their market share. Coca-Cola buyers also buy Pepsi at rates predictable from Pepsi's market share. This means brands do not have unique customer bases; they share a common pool of category buyers. The implication: segmentation and targeting based on brand loyalty are largely futile because "loyal" customers are mostly just category-heavy buyers.

### 5. Light Buyers Matter Most
The majority of a brand's customers are light buyers who purchase infrequently. These light buyers collectively contribute a substantial share of volume. Marketing that reaches only heavy buyers misses the largest source of growth potential. Broad reach strategies that touch light and non-buyers are more effective than narrow targeting of heavy buyers.

### 6. Distinctive vs. Differentiated
Sharp draws a crucial distinction: brands need to be distinctive (recognizable and identifiable) more than differentiated (offering unique functional or emotional benefits). Consumers rarely perceive meaningful differences between competing brands. What matters is being recognizable and easy to recall. Investment should go toward building distinctive brand assets, not toward crafting differentiated positioning statements.

### 7. NBD-Dirichlet Model
The mathematical model underlying many of Sharp's empirical laws. It describes how buyers are distributed across brands in a category and predicts purchase patterns. The model shows that most brand-level metrics (penetration, purchase frequency, loyalty, share of wallet) are largely determined by market share and category structure, not by brand-specific factors. Deviations from the model's predictions are where genuine strategic insights lie.

### 8. Category Entry Points (CEPs)
The mental situations or cues that trigger category buying behavior. A person might think of coffee when they are tired, when it is morning, when they smell it, or when they are meeting a friend. Brands grow by linking themselves to more CEPs, making them mentally available in more buying situations. Measuring and expanding CEP coverage is a key growth strategy.

## Application to Data Squad

- **Penetration vs. loyalty diagnostics:** When analyzing client customer bases, distinguish between penetration-driven growth and loyalty-driven growth. Sharp's research suggests penetration is usually the bigger lever, which challenges many clients' loyalty-focused strategies.
- **Mental and physical availability metrics:** Help clients measure and track mental availability (brand recall in context, CEP coverage) and physical availability (distribution, findability, purchase friction) alongside traditional brand metrics.
- **Light buyer analysis:** Ensure client analytics do not focus exclusively on heavy/loyal customers. Build analyses that quantify the contribution of light buyers and the opportunity to convert non-buyers.
- **Double jeopardy benchmarking:** Use the double jeopardy law to set realistic expectations for loyalty metrics. If a small brand's loyalty metrics look low, it may be a market share effect rather than a brand problem.
- **Productive tension with Fader:** Sharp and Fader disagree about the relative importance of penetration vs. CLV. Use this tension productively: Sharp's framework guides acquisition strategy (reach broadly), while Fader's guides investment allocation (invest differentially).
- **Distinctive asset tracking:** Help clients audit and measure the strength of their distinctive brand assets across touchpoints.

## Key Quotes

> "Loyalty is largely a function of market share, not a cause of it. Brands do not grow by increasing loyalty; they grow by increasing penetration."

> "The biggest myth in marketing is that there is a meaningful group of loyal customers who are fundamentally different from everyone else. There is not."

> "Mental availability is not awareness. Awareness is knowing a brand exists. Mental availability is thinking of that brand in the moment when you are ready to buy."
