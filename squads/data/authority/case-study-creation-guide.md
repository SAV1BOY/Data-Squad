# How to Create Compelling Data Case Studies

## Purpose

Data case studies are the most powerful tool for demonstrating analytical impact, building credibility with stakeholders, and creating institutional memory. This guide provides a step-by-step process for creating case studies that are compelling, credible, and actionable.

## The Case Study Structure

Every data case study should follow this structure, which mirrors how decision-makers think:

### 1. The Situation (10% of content)
Set the scene. What was the business context? What problem was the organization facing? What was at stake?

**How to write it:**
- Lead with the business problem, not the data problem
- Quantify the stakes: "Customer churn had increased from 5% to 8% monthly, threatening $2.4M in annual revenue"
- Name the decision-maker and their concern (with permission or anonymized)
- Keep it to 2-3 paragraphs maximum

**Example:** "In Q3, the product team noticed that 30-day retention for new users had dropped from 42% to 35% over two quarters. With 10,000 new users per month, this represented approximately 700 additional lost users monthly, each with an estimated CLV of $180. The VP of Product asked the data team to diagnose the cause and recommend a fix."

### 2. The Approach (20% of content)
Describe what you did and why. This is where you demonstrate analytical rigor.

**How to write it:**
- Explain the analytical approach in plain language first, then add technical detail
- Justify your method: why this approach and not alternatives?
- Be honest about constraints: data limitations, time pressure, sample size challenges
- Show the analytical journey, including dead ends and pivots

**What to include:**
- Data sources used and any quality considerations
- Methodology (cohort analysis, experimentation, statistical modeling, etc.)
- Key assumptions and how you validated them
- Timeline from question to insight

### 3. The Insight (30% of content)
Present what you found. This is the core of the case study.

**How to write it:**
- Lead with the insight in one sentence, then support it with evidence
- Use visualizations strategically: one great chart beats five mediocre ones
- Show the "aha moment" -- the point where the data revealed something non-obvious
- Quantify the insight: how big is the effect, how confident are you?

**Visualization principles for case studies:**
- Use before/after comparisons where possible
- Annotate charts with the key insight (do not make the reader figure it out)
- Show uncertainty: confidence intervals, ranges, or scenario analysis
- Use consistent formatting across all visuals in the case study

### 4. The Action (20% of content)
Describe what was done as a result of the insight.

**How to write it:**
- Connect the insight directly to the decision or action taken
- Describe the implementation: what changed in the product, process, or strategy?
- Note any resistance or debate and how it was resolved
- Credit the decision-makers who acted on the data

### 5. The Impact (15% of content)
Quantify the result. This is what makes the case study credible and memorable.

**How to write it:**
- Measure the actual outcome, not the projected outcome
- Use the same metrics from the Situation section to show the before/after
- Be honest about attribution: did the data insight cause the improvement, or was it one of several factors?
- Include a timeline: how long did it take to see results?

**Example:** "After implementing the revised onboarding flow, 30-day retention recovered from 35% to 44% over the following quarter -- exceeding the original baseline of 42%. At 10,000 new users per month, this represented approximately 900 additional retained users monthly, with an estimated annual revenue impact of $1.9M."

### 6. The Learning (5% of content)
What did the team learn that applies beyond this specific case?

**How to write it:**
- Distill 2-3 generalizable lessons
- Connect to broader principles or frameworks
- Note what you would do differently next time

## Finding Case Study Material

### Where to look in your own work
- Experiments that changed product direction
- Analyses that prevented bad decisions (the "we almost did X" stories)
- Data quality improvements that changed metric interpretation
- Dashboard or metric redesigns that changed how teams operate
- Predictions that proved accurate (or instructively inaccurate)

### The "saved the company from a bad decision" angle
These are often the most compelling case studies. The format is: "The team was about to do X. Data showed Y. The decision was changed to Z, avoiding [quantified negative outcome]."

### Building the habit
- At the end of every major analysis, spend 30 minutes documenting the case study while it is fresh
- Maintain a case study backlog: a list of potential case studies with a sentence about why each is interesting
- Schedule monthly "case study writing" time on the team calendar

## Formatting and Presentation

### For written case studies (blog posts, documents)
- Length: 800-1,500 words
- Include 2-3 key visuals
- Use headers and bullet points for scannability
- End with a clear "bottom line" statement

### For presentation case studies (slides, talks)
- Length: 5-10 slides or 10-15 minutes speaking
- One insight per slide, heavily visual
- Tell the story chronologically: situation -> approach -> insight -> action -> impact
- End with the generalizable lesson

### For stakeholder case studies (executive summaries)
- Length: 1 page maximum
- Lead with impact, then explain how you got there
- Include one visual maximum
- End with the decision that was made and its result

## Quality Checklist

Before publishing or presenting a case study, verify:

- [ ] The business problem is clearly stated and quantified
- [ ] The methodology is explained in accessible language
- [ ] The insight is non-obvious (not just "we confirmed what everyone assumed")
- [ ] The impact is measured with actual data, not projections
- [ ] Attribution is honest (you did not overclaim causation)
- [ ] Visualizations are clear, labeled, and annotated
- [ ] The generalizable lesson is articulated
- [ ] Sensitive data has been anonymized or approved for sharing
- [ ] A colleague has reviewed for clarity and accuracy
- [ ] The case study passes the "so what?" test -- a reader who knows nothing about the project would find it valuable

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Leading with methodology | Analysts find methods interesting | Lead with the business problem and stakes |
| Overclaiming causation | Desire to show impact | Be precise: "associated with" vs. "caused" |
| Burying the insight | Following chronological order too rigidly | State the key finding in the first paragraph |
| Skipping the impact section | Results take time to materialize | Wait to publish until you have measured results |
| Making it too technical | Writing for analysts, not decision-makers | Write for the smartest non-analyst in the room |
| Forgetting the human element | Focusing only on data | Include the decision, the debate, and the people involved |

## Building a Case Study Library

- Store case studies in a central, searchable location
- Tag them by: methodology used, business function served, metric category, outcome type
- Review and refresh annually: update impact numbers, retire outdated cases
- Use the library for onboarding new team members, stakeholder education, and recruiting materials
