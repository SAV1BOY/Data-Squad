# Davenport: Competing on Analytics Framework

## Overview

Thomas Davenport's "Competing on Analytics" (2007, updated 2017) argues that the most successful companies differentiate themselves not through products or brand alone, but through their superior use of data and analytics. Davenport provides an analytics maturity model and a roadmap for organizations seeking to make analytics a strategic capability rather than a support function.

For data squads, this framework answers the organizational question: where are we on the analytics maturity curve, and what must change to advance? It addresses the people, process, and culture dimensions that determine whether analytics investments produce results or gather dust.

## Origin

Thomas Davenport is a professor at Babson College and a senior advisor to Deloitte Analytics. His research spans decades of studying how organizations extract value from information. "Competing on Analytics" was based on a multi-year study of organizations that had made analytics a core competitive capability, including Capital One, Amazon, Netflix, and Harrah's Entertainment (now Caesars).

The book arrived at a moment when organizations were drowning in data but struggling to convert it into decisions. Davenport's contribution was framing analytics not as a technology problem but as a strategic management challenge.

## Key Concepts

### The Analytics Maturity Model (DELTA)

Davenport's DELTA model assesses organizational analytics readiness across five dimensions:

**D - Data:** Is the organization's data clean, integrated, and accessible? Maturity progresses from siloed spreadsheets to unified, governed data assets.

**E - Enterprise:** Is analytics a local initiative or an enterprise-wide strategic capability? Mature organizations have centralized analytics leadership with distributed execution.

**L - Leadership:** Does senior leadership understand and champion analytics? Analytical competitors have C-suite executives who set the analytical agenda and allocate resources accordingly.

**T - Targets:** Does the organization apply analytics to strategic, high-value targets? Immature organizations apply analytics to whatever is convenient; mature ones focus on the decisions with the largest financial impact.

**A - Analysts:** Does the organization attract, develop, and retain analytical talent? This includes not just data scientists but also analytical translators who bridge technical and business domains.

### Five Stages of Analytics Maturity

1. **Analytically Impaired** -- Data is siloed, inconsistent, and rarely used for decisions. Reports are manual and backward-looking. Most decisions are based on intuition or hierarchy.

2. **Localized Analytics** -- Individual teams or departments have adopted analytics, but there is no coordination. Duplicate metrics, conflicting definitions, and tool proliferation are common.

3. **Analytical Aspirations** -- The organization recognizes the strategic value of analytics and begins investing in infrastructure, talent, and governance. A centralized data team may be forming.

4. **Analytical Companies** -- Analytics is embedded in major business processes. There are enterprise-wide data standards, a mature data platform, and analytics informs most strategic decisions.

5. **Analytical Competitors** -- Analytics is the primary basis of competition. The organization continuously innovates its analytical capabilities and uses data advantages to outperform competitors.

### Strategic vs. Operational Analytics

Davenport distinguishes between strategic analytics (informing major business decisions like market entry, pricing strategy, product portfolio) and operational analytics (optimizing daily processes like supply chain, fraud detection, customer routing). Both are valuable, but organizations often invest only in operational analytics because the ROI is easier to measure, leaving strategic decisions unserved.

### The Analytical Translator Role

One of Davenport's most practical insights is the need for "analytical translators" -- people who understand both business context and analytical methods well enough to bridge the two. Without translators, data scientists build technically correct but business-irrelevant analyses, and business leaders make requests that are analytically incoherent.

### Analytics as Culture, Not Just Capability

Davenport emphasizes that technology and talent are necessary but insufficient. An analytics culture requires:
- Leaders who ask "what does the data say?" before making decisions
- Incentive structures that reward evidence-based decisions over gut instinct
- Tolerance for experimental failure combined with intolerance for ignoring available data
- Organizational processes that create space for analytical input before decisions are finalized

## Application to Data Squad

### Maturity Assessment

Use the DELTA model to assess the organization's current analytics maturity. This provides an honest baseline and prevents the data squad from attempting Stage 5 work in a Stage 2 organization. Conduct the assessment annually and track progress.

### Prioritize High-Value Targets

Apply Davenport's "targets" principle: identify the business decisions with the highest financial impact and focus analytical resources there first. A common failure is spreading the data team thin across many low-value requests while ignoring the decisions that move millions.

### Build the Translator Layer

Invest in analytical translators -- either by hiring for the role or by training existing business analysts and product managers to serve as bridges. The data squad cannot scale if every business question must flow through a data scientist.

### Enterprise Data Standards

Push for enterprise-wide metric definitions, data quality standards, and tool consolidation. Stage 2 (localized analytics) organizations suffer from conflicting metrics and duplicated work. The data squad can lead the standardization effort.

### Demonstrate Strategic Value

Actively seek strategic analytics opportunities, not just operational ones. Operational analytics keeps the lights on; strategic analytics gets the data team a seat at the leadership table.

### Leadership Education

Proactively educate senior leaders on analytics capabilities and limitations. Leaders who understand what analytics can and cannot do make better requests and better decisions. This is an investment in the "Leadership" dimension of DELTA.

## Pitfalls

### Technology-First Thinking

Davenport explicitly warns against starting with technology. Organizations that buy a "data platform" before defining their analytical strategy end up with expensive infrastructure and no impact. Strategy first, technology second.

### Skipping Maturity Stages

Attempting Stage 5 analytics (advanced AI, real-time optimization) in a Stage 2 organization (no data governance, no standard definitions) will fail. Each stage builds on the previous one. Data squads must be honest about where the organization is and work incrementally.

### Undervaluing the Human Element

Analytics maturity is at least as much about people and culture as it is about technology. An organization with mediocre tools but strong analytical culture will outperform one with world-class tools and no analytical culture.

### The "Center of Excellence" Trap

Centralizing all analytics into a Center of Excellence can create a bottleneck. Davenport advocates for a hub-and-spoke model: centralized standards and infrastructure, distributed analytical talent embedded in business units.

### Ignoring Change Management

Moving up the maturity curve requires changing how people work and make decisions. This is organizational change management, and it needs deliberate effort -- stakeholder engagement, training, incentive alignment, and patience.
