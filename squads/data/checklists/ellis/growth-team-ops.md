# Growth Team Ops

## Purpose

Audit the operational health of the growth team: its routines, documentation practices, decision-making processes, and retrospective habits. A growth team's output is a function of its operating system. Ellis-style growth requires disciplined execution, not just good ideas.

---

## Checklist Items

### 1. A Weekly Growth Meeting Exists with Consistent Format
- **Pass:** The team meets weekly with a standing agenda: review last week's experiment results, discuss active experiments, prioritize upcoming experiments, and share learnings. Minutes are recorded.
- **Fail:** Meetings are ad hoc, have no consistent agenda, or are frequently skipped.

### 2. Meeting Attendance Is Cross-Functional
- **Pass:** The growth meeting includes product, engineering, design, data/analytics, and marketing representation. Decisions can be made without waiting for absent stakeholders.
- **Fail:** The meeting is product-only or marketing-only, creating handoff delays and siloed thinking.

### 3. Experiment Documentation Is Standardized
- **Pass:** A template exists for experiment documentation (hypothesis, setup, metrics, results, learnings) and is used consistently. All experiments are logged regardless of outcome.
- **Fail:** Documentation is inconsistent -- some experiments are detailed, others are a Slack message, and many are undocumented.

### 4. A Shared Learning Repository Exists
- **Pass:** All experiment results and learnings are stored in a searchable repository (Notion, wiki, database). New team members can find past learnings without asking.
- **Fail:** Learnings are scattered across Slack, email, Google Docs, and individual memories.

### 5. Retrospectives Are Held Regularly
- **Pass:** The team holds a retrospective at least monthly, reviewing what worked, what did not, and what to change in process. Action items from retros are tracked.
- **Fail:** No retrospectives are held, or they are held but produce no action items.

### 6. Decision-Making Authority Is Clear
- **Pass:** It is documented who can approve experiment launches, who can call experiments, and who makes prioritization decisions. These roles are understood by all team members.
- **Fail:** Every decision requires escalation, or decisions are made by whoever is most vocal in the moment.

### 7. Capacity Planning Accounts for Experimentation
- **Pass:** Sprint or cycle planning explicitly allocates capacity for growth experiments (typically 20-40% of engineering time for growth-focused squads).
- **Fail:** Growth experiments compete with feature work without dedicated allocation, and experiments lose every time.

### 8. Onboarding for New Team Members Is Defined
- **Pass:** A new growth team member can get up to speed within 2 weeks using documented onboarding materials: team charter, experiment history, current backlog, tool access, and process guides.
- **Fail:** New team members learn by osmosis, taking months to become productive.

### 9. Metrics Are Accessible to the Whole Team
- **Pass:** Key growth metrics (NSM, activation rate, experiment results) are visible on a shared dashboard that the entire team can access without requesting data pulls.
- **Fail:** Only the data person can see the metrics, creating a bottleneck for information.

### 10. The Team Has a Documented Charter
- **Pass:** The growth team has a written charter stating its mission, scope, metrics it owns, relationship to other teams, and decision rights.
- **Fail:** The team's purpose and boundaries are undefined, leading to scope creep or conflicts with other teams.
